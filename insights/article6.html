What Fees Do Space Funds Charge, and What Do Investors Get for Them?

space fund fees · 10 min read

In the spring of 2026, the chief investment officer of a Midwestern family office put two documents side by side on her desk. One was the prospectus for a space ETF. The other was the limited partnership agreement for a space venture fund. Both promised a share of the orbital economy. One charged 0.75 percent a year; the other charged 2 percent a year, plus a fifth of the profits. On $10 million, the gap was worth about $1.25 million in management fees alone over a decade. The question was the one every space investor eventually faces: what, exactly, is the expensive version buying?

The answer starts with a fact most first-time space investors miss. There is no single price for space exposure. There are at least three, differing by a factor of twenty-five. The cheapest way in is an index fund. The dearest is a private venture fund. In between sits the world’s only listed space fund, which publishes its fee schedule for anyone to read. The spread is not a rounding error. It is the whole investment thesis, stated in basis points.

Two prices for the same theme

Start with the public market, where the fees are printed on the prospectus. The two largest space ETFs — Procure’s UFO, which tracks an index of space-revenue companies, and ARK Invest’s ARKX, Cathie Wood’s actively managed space and defence fund — each charge 0.75 percent a year. The SPDR S&P Kensho Final Frontiers ETF, ROKT, charges 0.45 percent. VistaShares’ GALX, launched in July 2026, charges 0.75 percent. On $10,000, the standard ticket costs $75 a year. Top Space ETFs’ October 2026 comparison, drawn from the issuers’ own fund pages, puts the three side by side, with ROKT the outlier at 0.45.

Set that against the baseline of American investing. Vanguard’s S&P 500 ETF, VOO, charges 0.03 percent a year — $3 on $10,000 — under its April 2026 prospectus. The space ETFs cost twenty-five times as much. That is the first premium a space investor pays, and it is worth pausing on, because it buys no stock-picking and, in UFO’s case, no active management at all. It buys the theme: an index of space companies is a small, illiquid, hard-to-trade corner of the market, and running it costs more than running an index of the five hundred largest American companies. Whether the theme is worth twenty-five times the price of the market is a separate question from whether the fund is well run. The fee only tells you what the idea costs to hold.

The venture bill: 2 and 20, and what it actually costs

Private space funds do not publish prospectuses. Their terms live in limited partnership agreements, and the market standard is the one venture capital has used for a generation: 2 and 20. Two percent a year in management fees, twenty percent of the profits as carried interest. Carta’s December 2025 Fund Economics Report, covering about 2,000 US funds, puts the median venture management fee at exactly 2 percent during the investment period, with step-downs in 82 percent of funds, and median carried interest at 20 percent, rising to 25 to 30 percent at the top decile. Hurdle rates — a minimum return investors must receive before carry starts — appear in only about 10 to 12 percent of venture funds. The median general partner commitment, the manager’s own money in the fund, is 1.7 percent. The standard term is ten years plus extensions. Kurums’ summary of the report lays out the market norms.

Two percent sounds modest until it is multiplied by time and by the fee base. The fee is usually charged on committed capital during the investment period, not on money actually invested — so the investor pays the full fee in year one even if the fund has called only a small part of the commitment. With step-downs, total fees over a fund’s life typically come to 10 to 15 percent of the commitment. Without them, a decade at 2 percent consumes close to a fifth of the commitment before a single dollar of profit is shared. On $10 million, 2 percent is $200,000 a year, in good years and bad, while the companies are still building hardware that has not flown. Carry, by contrast, is worth nothing unless the fund returns more than it raised. The fee is certain; the carry is contingent. The fee is what the manager lives on; the carry is what the manager gets rich on.

Space funds sit at the expensive end of that standard, and the reason is structural. A space venture fund is small — Seraphim Space’s latest early-stage fund, closed in February 2026 above its $100 million target, is typical of the category — and a 2 percent fee on a $100 million fund is $2 million a year to pay a team that must judge propulsion systems, satellite buses, and spectrum licences. Rent and salaries do not shrink in proportion to fund size. The investor is paying for a team that can tell a real constellation from a rendering, and there are not many such teams.

The one listed space fund that shows its workings

There is exactly one place where a space fund’s fees are public, audited, and argued over in the open: the London Stock Exchange. The Seraphim Space Investment Trust, launched in July 2021 as the world’s first listed SpaceTech fund, charges a management fee of 1.25 percent a year on net assets up to £300 million and 1 percent above that, plus a performance fee of 15 percent of returns above an 8 percent annual hurdle, subject to a high-water mark. Hargreaves Lansdown’s published fee structure for the trust states both figures. Ongoing charges fell to 1.36 percent in the year to June 2026, from 1.77 percent the year before, according to the annual report.

That year was the trust’s best. The underlying portfolio reached 359.6 percent of its original cost, its fair value rising 133 percent to £605.5 million, and the trust delivered a net asset value total return of 102.9 percent and a share price return of 119.4 percent. The five largest holdings accounted for 75 percent of fair value, and the top ten for 97 percent, with 93 percent of the portfolio in private companies. Fidelity’s October 2026 account of the annual report carries the figures. The performance fee provision in the accounts stood at £54 million — and the payout conditions had not yet been met.

Two details in that record matter more than the headline return. The first is concentration: a portfolio where five companies are three-quarters of value is a bet, not a basket, and the fee is being paid on a handful of judgments. The second is the discount. At 30 June 2026 the trust’s shares traded at a 22 percent discount to net asset value, down from 28 percent the year before. The market valued the same portfolio at roughly four-fifths of the price the manager’s own accounts put on it — a standing verdict on whether the fee, the illiquidity, and the concentration are worth it. The trust’s own target is annualised NAV returns of 20 percent over the long term. Whether that ambition survives a down year is the test the fee has not yet faced.

What the premium is supposed to buy

The case for paying venture fees in space rests on access. The companies that matter are private, the rounds are small, and the best of them are oversubscribed. Seraphim’s February 2026 fund close brought in the British Business Bank, the National Security Strategic Investment Fund, and corporate backers including Arabsat, Eutelsat, NEC, and SKY Perfect JSAT. The firm has backed 149 companies across 33 countries, nine of which have reached unicorn status, and its portfolio companies have collectively raised more than £10 billion. Dealroom’s report on the February 2026 close records the raise and the roster. A retail investor buying UFO gets the listed tail of the space economy. A limited partner in a Seraphim fund gets the private head — the rounds before the listing, at prices the public market never sees.

The second thing the premium buys is timing. Space Capital’s quarterly data shows $55.3 billion poured into 431 space companies in 2025, a 65 percent increase on 2024, with $17 billion of it arriving in the final quarter alone. Morningstar’s report on the Space Capital figures, published in January 2026, carries the totals. That flood is not evenly spread — late-stage mega-rounds took the bulk of it — and being inside the rounds that matter is the service a venture fee is meant to pay for. The counter to that claim is that the flood itself raises prices for everyone inside it, and a fund that pays up in a hot market is buying the same inflation as the public buyer, plus a fee.

The honest counterargument

The case against the premium is arithmetic, and it is brutal. Fees compound against the investor while returns compound for the manager only if the fund succeeds. A 2 percent fee with no step-down takes a fifth of the commitment over ten years. Carry takes a fifth of the profit above that. For the investor to match a plain index fund, the underlying companies must return well over twice the committed capital in gross terms — before the illiquidity, the concentration, and the decade of waiting. The ETF holder, by contrast, pays 0.75 percent, sells any day, and holds SpaceX itself: after the June 2026 listing, SpaceX became the largest holding in both UFO and ARKX. The public market, for once, is not the poor cousin of the private market. It holds the single most valuable space asset on earth, at a fraction of the venture fee, with a daily exit.

There is also the question of what the fee actually purchased in the last cycle. The 2021 SPAC wave took a generation of space companies public at valuations the public market then cut by 70 to 90 percent. The venture funds that fed that wave charged full fees on the way up. Their investors paid for access to rounds that turned out to be priced for a market that did not exist. None of that proves the fee is unjustified. It proves the fee is not a proxy for judgment: a high fee buys a team with opinions, not opinions that are right.

What it means for everyone else

Read the fee schedule before the pitch deck. Four questions settle most of it. What is the fee charged on — committed capital or invested capital — and when does it step down? Is there a hurdle before carry starts, and is carry calculated on the whole fund or deal by deal? What is the manager’s own commitment? And what are the fund expenses on top of the fee, and is there a cap? Carta’s data says hurdles are rare and step-downs are common but not universal. That gap is where the negotiation lives.

The deeper question is whether the premium is buying something that cannot be bought cheaper. If the answer is access to private rounds at prices the public market never sees, the venture fee can be justified — but only if the fund actually gets into those rounds, and only if the prices are genuinely better. If the answer is a theme and a basket of listed space names, the ETF at 0.75 percent already sells it, and the S&P 500 at 0.03 percent is the reminder that every basis point above the market has to earn its keep. The fee is the one number in a space fund that is known in advance. Returns are uncertain. The fee is not. Judge the fund by what it charges for certain, and make it prove the rest.
