Five Months a Public Company: What SpaceX’s Listing Has Actually Settled

space fund performance · 5 min read

Five months after SpaceX started trading on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, the argument about the company has moved from the roadshow to the filings — and the filings are less generous than the roadshow was. The listing settled what the market will pay. It did not settle what the market is paying for.

The listing facts are now a matter of record. SpaceX filed confidentially with the SEC on April 1, 2026, in an offering internally codenamed Project Apex, and priced on June 11, 2026 at $135 a share, valuing the company at roughly $1.77 trillion and raising $75 billion — the largest IPO in history, surpassing Saudi Aramco’s 2019 record of $29.4 billion. Trading began on Nasdaq as SPCX on June 12, 2026. The company that listed was not the SpaceX of a year earlier: in February 2026 it had absorbed xAI, Elon Musk’s artificial intelligence company, in an all-stock transaction, adding AI compute and the X platform to a business built on launch and connectivity. The secondary-market path to the listing ran from a $350 billion valuation in mid-2025 to $800 billion in December 2025, then $1.25 trillion at the xAI merger. Value Add VC’s IPO timeline reconstructs the sequence from the secondary sales through the June debut; Trendy Tech Tribe’s IPO account records the June 11 pricing and the $75 billion raise.

The prospectus answered the biggest question and complicated the answer. Final prospectus figures put 2025 Connectivity revenue — Starlink — at $11.4 billion, below the roughly $15 billion estimates circulating when the listing was first reported. Combined 2025 revenue for the company came in at roughly $15.5 to $17 billion, with Starlink at about 80% of it. Starlink passed 10 million subscribers worldwide in February 2026, up from approximately 1 million just two years earlier, over a constellation of more than 6,000 satellites. Analysts project Starlink revenue reaching $15.9 to $24 billion by the end of 2026, depending on how fast international subscriber growth continues. Useluminix’s SpaceX company overview carries the segment figures and the subscriber trajectory.

The valuation question is where the argument now lives. At roughly $1.77 trillion against about $15.5 to $17 billion of 2025 revenue, the listing priced the combined company at roughly 103 times sales, on Fortuna Fund’s analysis of the filing. Morgan Stanley’s Adam Jonas, whose $300 price target framed the bull case, attributes roughly $8 per share — under 3 percent of his target — to the rocket launch business; the rest comes from Starlink connectivity, enterprise AI compute, and X/Grok. And the satellites themselves depreciate at roughly $7 billion a year — slightly more than Starlink’s entire 2025 EBITDA of about $6.5 billion, which means the connectivity business’s true operating profit, after replacing the constellation, is thinner than the headline margin suggests.

The IPO settled what SpaceX is worth to the market. It did not settle what SpaceX is.

The honest counterargument

The bears’ math assumes the company stays as it is, and SpaceX has never stayed as it is. Starlink’s subscriber count grew tenfold in two years, the combined entity now includes xAI and Grok, and the bull case needs Starship at more than 100 flights a year by 2028 with Starlink at 20 million-plus subscribers — numbers that looked implausible for the subscriber count two years ago as well. There is also a second listing in the argument: no standalone Starlink IPO has been announced, but analysts put the earliest realistic window at 2027 and beyond, with a possible carve-out within 36 months of the IPO at a $500 billion-plus standalone valuation. A Starlink spin-out would re-rate the parts and answer, in market prices, whether the connectivity business is worth more alone than inside the bundle.

What it means for everyone else

Every space company now has a public comp, and the comp says the market pays for connectivity and AI compute, not launch. Launch is a rounding error in the bull case. That reframes the supply-chain story: the $1 trillion-plus economy behind SpaceX — the satellite manufacturers, the ground stations, the component suppliers, the data businesses — is where the multiples now live, because it is where the revenue lives. The real work, as ever, is identifying the next hundred companies that turn orbital infrastructure into terrestrial revenue.

Watch the first full-year results as a public company, and watch for any Starlink carve-out signal. The listing gave the space economy a benchmark. The next twelve months will show whether the benchmark was a price or a peak.
