Space Funds vs. Aerospace ETFs: The Liquidity Trade-Off

space funds vs aerospace etfs · 7 min read

In 2022, the two funds that were supposed to be the same trade went in opposite directions. The iShares U.S. Aerospace & Defense ETF — ITA — fell 8% in a year when defence budgets were rising. The ARK Space Exploration & Innovation ETF — ARKX — fell 34.27%. Both were sold as ways to own the space economy. One owned the companies that build the hardware. The other owned the companies that promise to. The difference between the two is the liquidity trade-off, and it is the single most important fact about space investing that the marketing never mentions.

The numbers make the trade-off concrete. Over the five years to November 2026, ITA returned 15.25% a year; ARKX returned 5.88% a year, according to PortfoliosLab's comparison dated November 19, 2026. In 2025, the two funds were nearly identical — ITA up 48.64%, ARKX up 48.46% — which is exactly the kind of year that makes the difference look small. It is not small. ARKX's five-year maximum drawdown was 42.8%, against ITA's 20.0%. An investor who bought ARKX at its 2021 peak watched nearly half the position disappear before it came back. An investor who bought ITA watched a fifth disappear. The difference is what each fund owns, and what each fund can sell.

What each fund actually holds

ITA is, in substance, a defence ETF with a space label. Its largest holdings are GE Aerospace at roughly 20%, RTX at roughly 17%, and Boeing at roughly 7% — three of the largest, most liquid industrial companies in the world, each trading tens of millions of shares a day. The fund manages about $13.5 billion. Its expense ratio is 0.38%. When an investor sells ITA, the fund sells shares in companies that trade every second of every trading day, and the price the investor gets is the price on the screen.

ARKX is the opposite construction. Its largest holding is SpaceX at roughly 10.6% — a private company, with no public market, whose shares trade only in periodic tender offers at prices set by the company. Its next holdings are L3Harris at 7.5% and Kratos at 6.2% — mid-cap defence and space companies that are liquid, but not in the same league as GE or RTX. The fund manages about $0.7 billion, roughly a twentieth of ITA's size. Its expense ratio is 0.75%, nearly double ITA's. When an investor sells ARKX, the fund must sell what it can sell — the public holdings — because the private holding cannot be sold on demand. In a falling market, the liquid holdings go first, and the private holding becomes a larger share of what remains.

The private-company problem

SpaceX is the test case for the whole argument. It is the most valuable private company in the space sector, the largest holding in the purest space fund, and — as of April 2026 — in the process of preparing an initial public offering. Until that IPO happens, ARKX's SpaceX position is valued by the fund's board, not by a market. The valuation is an estimate, revised when a tender offer clears, and the fund's published net asset value carries that estimate as if it were a price. It is not a price. It is an opinion, and it is an opinion that has moved in only one direction for most of the last three years.

The same problem appears, in a different form, in the small-cap space names. Rocket Lab's shares closed at a then-record $20.72 in September 2021, fell 83% to $3.47 in April 2024, and reached roughly $100 in January 2026 — nearly thirty times the low, inside four and a half years. A fund that holds Rocket Lab owns a position whose value can change by an order of magnitude faster than the fund can rebalance it. The Procure Space ETF — UFO, the third space ETF — fell 25.88% in 2022, in the same year ARKX fell 34.27%. The pattern is consistent: the purer the space exposure, the larger the drawdown, and the less liquid the holdings, the harder it is to get out.

The counterargument, and where it holds

The case for the space fund is that the aerospace ETF is not a space fund at all. ITA's largest holdings are engine makers, missile makers, and airframers — companies whose revenue comes from commercial aviation and defence, not from space. An investor who buys ITA for space exposure is buying a defence fund with a space label, and the 15.25% a year it returned over five years came mostly from the defence cycle, not from the space economy. ARKX, for all its volatility, is the only ETF that actually owns the space economy — SpaceX, the satellite builders, the launch companies, the ground-station operators. The 5.88% a year it returned is the return on the space economy itself, and the 42.8% drawdown is the price of owning it early.

That argument has real force, and it is the reason ARKX exists. But it has a limit, and the limit is the one the numbers show: the investor who bought ARKX in 2021 and held through the drawdown earned 5.88% a year, while the investor who bought ITA earned 15.25% a year, and the investor who bought ARKX earned it with twice the volatility and twice the fee. The space economy's return, as measured by its purest fund, was lower than the defence cycle's return, as measured by its broadest fund — and the gap is not small. It is nearly ten percentage points a year, compounded over five years.

The honest reading is that the trade-off is not between space and defence. It is between liquidity and purity, and the investor should choose knowingly. A portfolio that can hold through a 42.8% drawdown, that does not need to sell in a crisis, and that wants the purest available exposure to the space economy should own ARKX — and should size the position for the drawdown, not for the upside. A portfolio that needs to sell on demand, that cannot tolerate a 40% decline, or that wants the space theme without the private-company risk should own ITA — and should be honest that it is buying defence, not space. The worst outcome is the investor who buys ARKX for ITA's liquidity, or buys ITA for ARKX's purity, and discovers the mistake in the year the market falls.

For a fund manager, the practical conclusion is to treat the space ETF and the aerospace ETF as different asset classes, not as substitutes: one is a liquid, low-fee, defence-tilted holding; the other is an illiquid, higher-fee, venture-tilted holding that happens to trade on an exchange. Size them accordingly, and do not rebalance one into the other on the basis of a single year's return. For an allocator, it is to ask, before buying a space fund, what share of the portfolio is private, how it is valued, and what happens to the fund's liquidity when the public holdings have to be sold to meet redemptions. And for anyone building a space fund, the missing product is now obvious. A fund that holds the pure space names with a liquidity buffer — cash, or a sleeve of liquid large-caps — and discloses the private valuation honestly, rather than carrying it at the last tender price, will own the next decade of space investing. The trade-off is real. The fund that manages it, rather than hiding it, wins.
