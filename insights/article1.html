Investing in Space: Public vs. Private Market Access Points

investing in space economy · 4 min read

In 2021, at the peak of the space SPAC frenzy, eleven space companies went public through reverse mergers. Combined, they raised over $7 billion. By 2023, most had lost 70 to 90 percent of their value. Virgin Orbit went bankrupt. Astra was taken private at a fraction of its SPAC valuation. The lesson seemed obvious: space investing was a bubble, and the bubble had burst.

The lesson was wrong. What burst was the SPAC structure, not the space economy. While the de-SPAC disasters dominated headlines, SpaceX was launching a rocket every four days, Starlink was adding a million subscribers a quarter, and private space investment was quietly setting records. The investors who understood the difference between the access point and the asset made fortunes. The ones who confused the two got the lesson they deserved.

The Public Markets: Limited but Liquid

Public market access to the space economy remains surprisingly thin. The pure-play options are few: Rocket Lab (RKLB), the most credible small-launch public company, which has clawed its way toward profitability on Electron launches and its growing Space Systems division. Planet Labs (PL), the earth-observation constellation operator. Spire Global (SPIR), in maritime and weather data. AST SpaceMobile (ASTS), betting on direct-to-cell satellite broadband — the highest-risk, highest-reward public space equity.

Then there are the aerospace primes with space exposure buried inside diversified businesses: Lockheed Martin, Northrop Grumman, L3Harris. Their space divisions are real — Northrop's space systems segment alone generates over $3 billion annually — but buying Lockheed for space exposure is like buying Berkshire for its Dairy Queen.

Thematic ETFs (ARKX, UFO) offer diversified exposure but suffer from the fundamental problem of thematic ETFs: they must own what is public, and the best space companies are private. ARKX's top holdings have routinely included companies with tangential space exposure (Trimble, Kratos) because the pure-play universe is too small to fill a fund.

"Public space equities are the tip of an iceberg that's 90% underwater. The listed companies are real, but they represent a fraction of where value is being created."

For most investors, public space equities serve a specific purpose: liquid, tactical exposure to the sector's sentiment cycle. They are not — with the possible exception of Rocket Lab — the primary vehicle for capturing the space economy's growth.

The Private Markets: Where the Value Lives

The real space economy is private. SpaceX, valued above $350 billion in late 2025 secondary transactions, is the most valuable private company in the world — and it is a space company. Blue Origin, backed by Jeff Bezos's effectively unlimited capital, is building orbital infrastructure at a pace no public company could sustain. Relativity Space, Stoke Space, and ABL Space Systems are attacking launch from different angles. Axiom Space is building the first commercial space station. Varda Space Industries is manufacturing pharmaceuticals in microgravity.

Accessing these companies requires private market infrastructure. The channels:

Space-focused venture funds. A small but growing set of managers specialize: Space Capital (Chad Anderson's firm, among the earliest dedicated space VCs), Embedded Ventures (Jordan Noone and Jenna Bryant, backing dual-use space tech), Type One Ventures, and 7Percent Ventures. These funds offer diversified exposure to 20 to 40 space startups — the closest thing to an index fund for private space.

Secondary transactions. Pre-IPO secondary markets (Forge, EquityZen, and the institutional secondary desks) periodically offer SpaceX, Stripe-style liquidity in late-stage space companies. SpaceX secondaries have been the defining trade of the private space market — each tender offer repricing higher, each one oversubscribed.

Direct co-investment. For family offices and institutions with space expertise, direct investments alongside venture funds offer the highest return potential and the highest diligence burden. The check sizes ($5 to $25 million) put this out of reach for most, but the return dispersion in space venture is extreme enough that manager selection matters less than company selection — one SpaceX covers a hundred failures.

The Access Decision

The choice between public and private space exposure is not really a choice — it is a portfolio construction question. Public equities provide liquidity, mark-to-market pricing, and the ability to trade around sentiment. Private investments provide access to the companies actually building the orbital economy, with the illiquidity and J-curve that entails.

Sophisticated space investors hold both: a liquid public sleeve for tactical positioning and a private sleeve — typically through specialized venture funds — for the structural growth. The allocation between them depends on liquidity needs, not on views about the sector. If you believe in the space economy's growth, you want maximum private exposure your liquidity constraints allow. The public names are what you own because you must, not because they are the best companies.

The SPAC era taught the market to distinguish the vehicle from the destination. The destination — a trillion-dollar orbital economy — is arriving on schedule. The vehicles for getting there are better understood now. Choose accordingly.
