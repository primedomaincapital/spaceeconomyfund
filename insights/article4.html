SpaceX Is Now a Stock. What That Means for Everyone Else.

space fund performance · 4 min read

When SpaceX started trading on Nasdaq under the ticker SPCX, the opening price was $150. Within weeks it had touched $225. Morgan Stanley’s Adam Jonas — the same analyst who spent years modeling Tesla’s robotaxi future — slapped a $300 price target on it, implying a valuation north of $4 trillion. His bull case: $600, or $8 trillion, making SpaceX the most valuable company in human history.

Read the fine print of Jonas’s model and something strange emerges. He attributes roughly $8 per share — under 3 percent of his $300 target — to SpaceX’s rocket launch business. The rockets, the thing the company is named for, are a rounding error. The other 97 percent comes from Starlink connectivity ($118), enterprise AI compute ($165), and X/Grok ($8). Morgan Stanley is valuing SpaceX as an AI infrastructure company that happens to own rockets, not a rocket company that happens to do other things.

This is either visionary or insane. Possibly both. But it frames the central question for space economy investors in 2026: are you betting on SpaceX, or are you betting on space?

The $1 trillion consensus (and why it’s wrong)

For years, the space economy’s bull case rested on a familiar set of forecasts. Morgan Stanley’s earlier work projected the global space industry reaching $1 trillion by 2040. Bank of America, Citi, and others published similar numbers. The logic was straightforward: launch costs falling (thanks to reusability), satellite constellations proliferating, new applications emerging.

Those forecasts are now being revised — not because they were too optimistic, but because they miscategorized the growth. The traditional “space economy” — launch services, satellites, ground equipment — is indeed growing. But the explosive value creation is happening in the layers above: the data, connectivity, and compute that space infrastructure enables. SpaceX’s valuation tells this story in miniature. The market isn’t paying $4 trillion for rockets. It’s paying for what rockets make possible.

Starlink is the clearest example. With thousands of satellites in orbit, SpaceX operates the largest satellite constellation in history. But Starlink’s value isn’t in the satellites — it’s in the connectivity revenue. Morgan Stanley’s $118-per-share attribution implies the market sees Starlink as a telecom company with orbital infrastructure, competing not with other satellite operators but with terrestrial broadband providers. That’s a much bigger market.

The AI compute angle is more speculative but potentially larger. Jonas models SpaceX deploying orbital data centers, leveraging Starship’s massive payload capacity to put compute in space at lower cost per watt than terrestrial alternatives. He assumes a 45 percent compound annual growth rate through 2040. If Starship works as advertised — and that’s a substantial if, with the vehicle still in test phase — the economics could be transformative. If it doesn’t, $165 of the $300 price target evaporates.

The supply chain nobody’s watching

While headlines fixate on SpaceX, the more interesting investment story may be the supply chain. Every satellite needs components. Every launch needs ground infrastructure. Every constellation needs spectrum coordination, debris tracking, and regulatory compliance.

Rocket Lab has carved out a position in small launch. Planet Labs operates the largest Earth observation constellation. Spire Global does maritime and weather data. AST SpaceMobile is building direct-to-device satellite connectivity. These are real companies with real revenue, trading at fractions of SpaceX’s implied multiples.

The defense angle adds another dimension. The Pentagon’s space budget continues growing, driven by the Space Development Agency’s proliferated constellation architecture and the recognition that space is now a warfighting domain. Companies with defense contracts — L3Harris, Northrop Grumman, Lockheed Martin’s space division — offer space exposure with government-backed revenue.

The counterargument to space enthusiasm is valuation discipline. SpaceX at $2 trillion market cap (recent trading levels) is priced for extraordinary execution over decades. The average analyst price target across 35 covering analysts is $217 — well below Jonas’s $300. Goldman Sachs’s Eric Sheridan sits at $230. There’s meaningful disagreement about whether the AI infrastructure narrative justifies current prices, especially with Starship not yet operational and the orbital data center concept unproven at scale.

There’s also the concentration risk. An investment thesis that depends on one company’s execution — however impressive that company — isn’t a space economy thesis. It’s a SpaceX thesis. The broader space economy includes hundreds of companies. Most won’t be worth trillions. Some will be worth billions. Identifying them requires looking past the halo.

What the fund manager should ask

The space economy fund that thrives won’t be the one with the biggest SpaceX position. It’ll be the one that understood the layer cake: infrastructure at the bottom (launch, satellites), services in the middle (connectivity, Earth observation, in-space manufacturing), and applications on top (the AI compute, the direct-to-device broadband, the use cases nobody’s imagined yet).

Each layer has different risk profiles, different time horizons, and different competitive dynamics. Launch is commoditizing — that’s good for everyone except launch providers. Connectivity is scaling — that’s good for operators with deployed constellations. Applications are where the uncapped upside lives, but also where the uncertainty is highest.

SpaceX going public didn’t create the space economy. It gave it a ticker symbol and a $2 trillion conversation starter. The real work — identifying the next hundred companies that turn orbital infrastructure into terrestrial revenue — is just beginning.
