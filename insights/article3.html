Space Fund Performance: Early Returns from Orbital Investing

space fund performance · 4 min read

Space venture capital is barely a decade old as a distinct category. That makes performance data scarce, noisy, and — in the hands of fund managers raising their next fund — selectively presented. But enough time has passed, and enough funds have matured, to draw preliminary conclusions about what works in orbital investing and what doesn't.

The early returns suggest something the skeptics missed: space venture, done correctly, looks less like science fiction and more like enterprise software venture — with comparable return profiles and surprisingly similar loss ratios.

What the First Generation Shows

Space Capital, founded by Chad Anderson in 2017 as one of the first dedicated space VCs, provides the longest track record. The firm's early funds backed companies including Rocket Lab (now public), Planet Labs (now public), Spire Global (now public), and ICEYE (the SAR constellation leader, still private). While full fund-level returns aren't public, the firm's public exits and markups suggest top-quartile venture performance — driven, notably, not by moonshots but by infrastructure: launch, earth observation, and communications.

The pattern repeats across the category. The best-performing space investments to date share three characteristics: they sell to governments or enterprises (not consumers), they generate revenue within three years of founding (not ten), and they benefit from the cost collapse in launch and manufacturing (the rising tide). Rocket Lab's Electron — 60+ successful launches — is a business, not a bet. Planet's daily earth imagery is a subscription product, not a science project.

"The best space investments don't look like space investments. They look like infrastructure businesses that happen to operate in orbit."

The failures are equally instructive. The space SPAC cohort of 2021 — Virgin Orbit, Astra, Momentus, Spire (which survived but barely) — shared the inverse characteristics: consumer or speculative revenue models, decade-long paths to profitability, and valuations set by SPAC sponsors rather than fundamentals. The lesson for fund investors: the vehicle matters as much as the sector. Space venture through disciplined VC funds has worked. Space exposure through SPACs was, with few exceptions, a wealth transfer from public investors to sponsors.

Benchmarking Against Venture Broadly

How does space venture compare to venture capital overall? The honest answer: it's too early for definitive benchmarking, but the leading indicators are encouraging.

Loss ratios appear comparable to enterprise software venture — roughly 40 to 50 percent of investments returning less than capital, which is normal for the asset class. The feared scenario (space startups failing at dramatically higher rates due to technical risk) hasn't materialized, largely because the technical risk has been retired: rockets work, satellites work, the physics is settled. The remaining risk is commercial — can the company sell? — which is the same risk as any startup.

Return dispersion is wider than average venture, driven by the extreme right tail. A single SpaceX — which many space funds hold through secondaries or early direct investments — can return an entire fund multiple times over. This is the power-law dynamic familiar to all venture investors, amplified: in space, the winners are not 10x outcomes but 100x outcomes, because the markets they create (global broadband, orbital manufacturing) are measured in hundreds of billions.

Time to liquidity is the genuine disadvantage. Space companies take longer to exit — 8 to 12 years versus 6 to 8 for software — because the capital requirements are larger and the strategic acquirer universe is smaller. Fund investors should underwrite longer fund lives and expect J-curves with deeper troughs. The managers who've navigated this (Space Capital's 10-year fund structures, Embedded Ventures' patient capital approach) understood the duration from inception.

What Fund Investors Should Demand

As the category matures, allocator diligence is getting more sophisticated — and more demanding. The questions that matter:

"What is your sourcing edge?" Space is a small community. The best deals are relationship-driven — founders who worked at SpaceX, JPL, or Rocket Lab and are known quantities to the ecosystem. A fund without deep network roots in the space community is paying retail for deals the insiders saw first.

"How do you underwrite technical risk?" The critical diligence question. A generalist VC can evaluate a SaaS company's unit economics. Evaluating a propulsion startup requires someone who understands specific impulse, mass fractions, and the difference between a test-stand firing and a flight-qualified engine. The credible space funds employ technical advisors or partners with genuine aerospace backgrounds — not consultants, but people who've built flight hardware.

"What is your reserve strategy?" Space companies are capital-intensive and often need multiple large rounds. A fund that invests $3 million in the seed round and has no reserves for the $30 million Series B is not a space investor — it's a lottery ticket buyer. Reserve ratios of 2:1 or 3:1 (reserves to initial investments) are appropriate for the category.

The first generation of space funds proved the category is investable. The second generation — now raising — will determine whether it's sustainably profitable. The early evidence favors the optimists, with the usual venture caveat: manager selection is everything, and the dispersion between the best and median space funds will likely exceed the dispersion in more mature venture categories.
